Commit
Objections, Decision Pull & the Full Thread

A diagnosis earns the right to a decision. Commit is where you collect it — by hearing objections as unfinished discovery, letting the decision pull instead of pushing, and holding the thread unbroken from the problem to a named, calendared commitment.

None of these are product problems. Commit is a discipline of finishing what discovery started — not a second attempt to persuade.

What commit is — and is not You are collecting a decision, not creating one

Commit is not
  • Persuading past a "no"
  • Discounting to force a "yes"
  • Chasing with follow-ups
  • Inventing a deadline
Commit is
  • Returning objections to their block
  • Letting the evaluation pull the decision
  • Replaying the thread the buyer already owns
  • Naming the decision, owner & date
Rule

If the diagnosis was real, the decision is already latent in the buyer’s own logic. Commit surfaces it — it does not manufacture it. When you feel yourself pushing, the thread has a gap; go find it.

What you will be able to do A performance module — outcomes, not vocabulary

Six outcomes around one verb
  • Hear any objection as a map back to an unclosed block
  • Handle it with Locate → Return → Re-confirm → Advance
  • Tell push from pull — and build the four sources of pull
  • Quantify a cost of delay from the buyer’s own numbers
  • Replay the full thread so the buyer confirms their own logic
  • End with a named, owned, calendared commitment

The final practice forbids any new claim, deadline or discount — commit is won by finishing the thread, not by adding to it.

The one sentence commit rests on Ownership is the whole game

“The decision-ready evaluation is already the buyer’s — their problem, their baseline, their acceptance test, their boundary, their path.”

Because the buyer owns it
  • Objections are theirs to resolve, not yours to defeat
  • Urgency comes from their consequence, not your quarter
  • The decision is theirs to make on the record you both hold
So the seller’s job is
  • Keep the record accurate and in front of them
  • Locate and close the last open block
  • Make the first step small enough to say yes to

Every objection is unfinished discovery Not an attack — a coordinate

An objection at commit tells you exactly which block of the diagnosis was never closed. It is a coordinate on the map, not a verdict on the deal.

Move

Do not answer the objection. Locate it on the map, return to that artifact, and let the buyer re-confirm it. The objection dissolves when the block closes.

An objection points backward to a block

The objection → block map Every common objection has a home in the diagnosis

What the buyer saysThe block it belongs toWhat was left open
“It isn’t really a priority right now.”A · Trigger & present stateThe trigger and present-state cost were never made vivid.
“How do we justify the spend?”B · Consequence & baselineThe consequence was never quantified against a baseline.
“How will we know it worked?”C · Outcome & acceptanceNo falsifiable acceptance test was agreed.
“Security / legal / procurement has to weigh in.”D · Boundary & decision pathThe operating boundary and decision path surfaced late.
“Let me think about it — send materials.”E · Fit & commitmentNo bounded, calendared next step was ever set.

The objection is not new information — it is the diagnosis telling you where it thinned out. Read it as a diagnostic, and the answer is already written.

Four kinds of objection Same words, different repair

Real
A genuine unclosed block. Repair: return to discovery on that block; a reopened block is progress, not loss.
Reflex
A habitual stall (“send me info”). Repair: name it gently and re-anchor to the agreed decision path and date.
Proxy
Someone else’s objection, voiced by your champion. Repair: surface the real owner via the decision path.
Price
Almost never about price. Repair: return to the consequence and the acceptance test — value is a baseline problem.

Misreading the kind is the error — rebutting a reflex, or discounting a real block. Name the kind first; the repair follows.

How to handle an objection Play it back to its block in four beats

1
Locate. Name the block the objection belongs to — out loud, without defensiveness.
2
Return. Go back to that artifact — the baseline, the acceptance test, the path — not to your pitch.
3
Re-confirm. Ask the buyer whether the artifact still holds. Their “yes” closes the block.
4
Advance. Return to the thread at the next open link — not to the start.

Playing an objection back A fill-in contract — the same shape every time

Objection playback — a grammar
“You’re right to raise [objection] — that lives in [block].
When we last spoke we agreed [the artifact].
Does that still hold, or has something changed?”
If it still holds
The block is closed by the buyer’s own words. Advance the thread.
If something changed
You just found live, un-diagnosed reality — return to discovery on that block. That is the deal getting more real, not less.

The playback never adds a claim. It only asks the buyer to reaffirm something they already told you.

The price objection is a baseline objection Discounting answers the wrong block

  • “It’s too expensive” is almost never about the number
  • It means the consequence of the problem was never quantified next to it
  • A discount confirms the price was the issue — and lowers your own baseline
  • The repair is Block B: put the cost of the problem and the acceptance test back on the table
Claim-safety

Quantify the consequence from the buyer’s own figures. Never invent a saving, an ROI multiple, or a benchmark to win the moment.

Return price to the baseline it belongs to

The “send me materials” reflex A soft stall answered with a hard anchor

The reflex answer

Send a deck, wait, chase, re-send. The thread goes cold and a new stakeholder reopens it.

→ decision never gets pulled
The anchored answer

“Happy to — what decision should those materials help you make, and by when?” Then set it on the calendar together.

→ the material serves a dated decision

Materials are never the point — the dated decision is. If the buyer can’t name the decision the materials serve, block E is still open.

When an objection is real A reopened block is the deal getting honest

Returning to discovery on a live block is not a step backward — it is newly discovered architecture. The alternative is a decision built on a gap.

Speed at commit is a false economy. A decision that survives its own objections is the only kind worth having.

Push versus pull Two ways a decision moves — only one holds

Push — the seller supplies the force
  • Deadlines, discounts, follow-ups, FOMO
  • Energy comes from you; resistance comes from them
  • Stops the moment you leave the room
Pull — the decision supplies its own force
  • Quantified consequence, buyer’s test, named path, cheap first step
  • Energy comes from their own logic
  • Keeps moving when you are not there

The four sources of pull Build these in discovery; collect them at commit

Quantified consequence
A cost of the problem — and of delay — in the buyer’s own numbers, with a clock.
The buyer’s acceptance test
A bar they wrote. People move toward a test they authored.
A named decision path
Who evaluates, approves, can veto — and by when. A path with a date pulls.
A cheap first step
A bounded next move that is easy to say yes to and hard to regret.
Diagnostic

If a decision is stalling, one of these four is missing — not the buyer’s will. Find the absent source and build it; do not add pressure.

Cost of delay is the honest clock The only urgency you never have to manufacture

  • Deciding later is itself a decision — with a running cost
  • Quantify what the problem spends every month it is unsolved, in the buyer’s figures
  • That number is the clock — it belongs to them, so it cannot be argued away
  • It replaces the fake deadline, the expiring discount, the “end of quarter”
The meter runs whether or not they decide

The pull check One question tells you whether the decision can stand on its own

“If I left the room right now — would the decision still move?”

Yes — pull is present
The four sources are built. Your job is to hold the thread and take the commitment.
No — you are the pull
The motion depends on your presence. A source is missing; return and build it before you ask for the decision.

Run the check silently before every commit ask. If the honest answer is “no,” asking now only teaches the buyer to stall.

Real versus manufactured urgency One earns trust; the other spends it

Real — keep it
  • The cost of delay from the buyer’s figures
  • A genuine, dated event in their world (a renewal, an audit, a launch)
  • A decision-path window that actually closes
Manufactured — never
  • Invented scarcity or a fake expiring price
  • “End of my quarter” dressed as their deadline
  • Pressure standing in for a missing source of pull
Claim-safety

Every deadline you cite must be true and the buyer’s own. A manufactured clock wins one meeting and loses the account.

The full thread The unbroken line from the problem to the commitment

The thread is the single source of truth you and the buyer both hold. Commit adds nothing to it — it just walks it, end to end, one more time.

The thread playback One narrative that hands the buyer their own logic

Thread playback — a grammar
“You told us [problem] is costing [baseline]. Success means [outcome], and we’ll know because [acceptance test]. It has to run inside [boundary], and [named people] decide by [date]. Have I got the thread right?”
Why it works

A buyer defends a conclusion they hear in their own words. The playback is not a summary of your pitch — it is a mirror of their decision. The ask that follows is theirs to grant.

Thread integrity What breaks the thread — and how to repair it

What breaks itHow it shows upThe repair
A new stakeholder“Let me loop in someone…” who never heard the threadReplay the thread for them first — never let a newcomer re-open closed blocks
A time gapWeeks pass; the consequence goes coldRe-confirm the baseline and the cost-of-delay before advancing
A champion changeYour owner leaves or is reassignedRe-establish the decision path; find who now owns the date
Scope creepNew requirements bolt on and blur the acceptance testReturn to Block C; re-agree the smallest falsifiable bar

A broken thread is the most common cause of a “stalled” deal. It is almost always repairable — by replaying, not restarting.

The commitment itself A decision is not a feeling — it has four fields

1
The decision. Named in one sentence — what is being decided, not “moving forward.”
2
The owner. A person, not a committee, accountable for it.
3
The date. When it is made — on both calendars, in the room.
4
The first step. The bounded, cheap-to-yes move that starts it now.
Calendared, in the room
“So the decision is [X]; [name] owns it; you’ll make it by [date]; and the first step is [bounded step]. Shall we put both on the calendar now?”

The decision-path playback, at commit Still the highest-value sentence in the deal

“[Names/roles] evaluate it, [name] approves it, [name] can stop it, and the decision is needed by [date]. Is that the real path?”

If any name or the date is still vague, block D is open — and a warm meeting is about to become a silent inbox.

A full-thread commit, worked Objection → block → return → pull → commitment

BeatWhat happensDoctrine
Objection“This is a lot of money — I’m not sure now’s the time.”Two objections: price (B) + timing (A).
Locate + return“Fair — when we scoped it, the backlog was costing ~[buyer’s figure] a month. Still true?”Return to baseline; buyer’s numbers only.
Re-confirm“Yes, if anything it’s worse.”Block B closes; cost-of-delay becomes the clock.
Pull“Your test was [acceptance test]; [name] approves; you wanted it before [event].”Three sources of pull, all the buyer’s.
Commitment“So: decide by [date], [name] owns it, first step is a bounded pilot. Calendar it?”Named, owned, dated, bounded.

No new claim, no discount, no invented deadline entered the conversation. The decision was collected, not created.

Failure modes Where sellers lose a decision they had already earned

Failure modeWhat it sounds likeCorrective move
Rebuttal“Actually, here’s why that’s not a problem…”Locate the block; return, don’t argue
Discount reflex“I could get you 10% off…”Return price to the baseline
Manufactured clock“This price expires Friday”Use the buyer’s cost of delay instead
Materials as progress“I’ll send a deck and follow up”Anchor to a dated decision first
Warm-and-vague close“Great chat — let’s stay in touch”Name decision, owner, date, first step

Commit does not eliminate judgment — it tells you where judgment belongs, and where improvisation quietly loses the deal.

The unifying line

You do not close a decision. You hold the thread until the buyer can see that the decision is already theirs to make.

Appendix A · facilitator answer key One objection, fully worked

Objection

“We love it, but let’s revisit next quarter.”

Read

Block A (timing) with a hidden Block D (an unnamed approver setting the quarter). Kind: proxy dressed as reflex.

Worked reply

“Makes sense — help me understand what changes next quarter. Is it budget timing, or does someone specific need to sign off? When we scoped it, the problem was costing [buyer’s figure] a month; that meter runs through the wait. Who owns the go/no-go, and could we calendar their decision now rather than a reminder later?”

Returns to A and D, uses the buyer’s own cost of delay, and converts a stall into a dated path — no new claim, no discount.

Appendix B · the thread template Fill it in during discovery; read it back at commit

The thread — one page you both hold
Problem ________ · costing [baseline] ________
Outcome ________ · proven by [acceptance test] ________
Boundary ________ · data / systems / actions that must stay ________
Decision path ________ evaluates · ________ approves · ________ can veto
Cost of delay ________ per month · Decision by ________
First step ________ (bounded, cheap to yes)

If a line is blank at commit, that is your open block. The template is the map, the playback script, and the commitment record in one.
